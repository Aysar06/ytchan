# Narration guide
**Written for:** faceless long-form business post-mortems · own voice · fluent non-native English (Lebanese English accent, kept deliberately) · never recorded before
**Built:** 2026-07-25, from research across delivery craft, long-form YouTube narration, non-native clarity, home recording, and writing-for-the-ear

---

## 0. The reframe — read this once and stop worrying about it

Pronunciation research separates three things people blur together:

- **Accentedness** — how different you sound from a local norm
- **Comprehensibility** — how much effort a listener spends
- **Intelligibility** — how much they actually understand

**They're only loosely correlated.** Heavily accented speech is routinely 100% intelligible; native-accented mumbling routinely isn't. So the accent is not the variable to move.

Two findings that should settle it:

1. **Listeners adapt to an unfamiliar accent within about a minute of exposure** — sometimes within two to four sentences — and the adaptation transfers to other speakers of the same accent. A returning subscriber is fully calibrated before your intro ends. **Your only real exposure window is the first 20–40 seconds of a new viewer's first video.** Put your clarity investment there, not spread thinly across 20 minutes.

2. **Jenkins' Lingua Franca Core** — research on English between non-native speakers — found a short list of features that carry nearly all the intelligibility load, and a longer list that language courses obsess over which barely matter. **"Th" sounds, dark /l/, and native vowel quality are on the doesn't-matter list.** That turns an infinite accent-erasure project into a punch list of about a dozen items (§7).

**The real risk isn't your accent. It's the flat, over-careful read** that comes from monitoring your own mouth while performing. Attention is zero-sum: self-monitoring competes with delivery, and it sounds like even stress, narrow pitch, no personality. The fix is workflow, not willpower — move every pronunciation decision into a markup pass *before* the mic, so that while recording you're only performing.

## 1. Your numbers ⭐

The research disagreed with itself here and the reconciliation matters, so this is the one section to get right.

One source said 140 WPM for documentary narration. Another **measured actual channels** and got much higher — Wendover 195, Modern MBA 193, Kurzgesagt 165, Real Engineering 165 (transcript word count ÷ runtime).

**Both are right, because they measure different things.** The 195 figure is *gross density* — words ÷ runtime with nearly all silence already removed in the edit. Wendover isn't speaking at 195; he's speaking around 165–175 and the gaps were cut out afterwards.

> **You reach the high density in the edit, not at the mic.**

Chasing 195 live produces exactly the rushed, clipped delivery that makes an accent harder to parse, and it destroys the pauses that are your single best clarity tool.

### Your targets

| | Value |
|---|---|
| **Record at** | **140–150 WPM** |
| **Write to** | **150 WPM** |
| **Effective density after silence trimming** | 160–170 WPM |
| 15-minute episode | ~2,250 words |
| 20-minute episode | ~3,000 words |
| **Episode 1 (see §10)** | **10–12 min ≈ 1,600 words** |

**Before you write anything**, record a 300-word passage and measure your actual rate. First-time non-native readers usually land 120–140 when being careful. Then adjust the *script length* to fit your natural pace — never force yourself to talk faster.

## 2. Session zero — 90 minutes, no script

**Episode one is not what you record first.** The first session produces a working setup and a calibration file. Nothing publishable. This is the highest-ROI 90 minutes in the project and it's the one everyone skips.

### Buy list (~$150–250, priority order)
1. **Samson Q2U** (~$70) — USB *and* XLR, dynamic, cardioid. The only mic to consider on day one: works today over USB, survives an upgrade later. Dynamic matters — it rejects room echo, which is what you have.
2. **Closed-back headphones** — ATH-M20x (~$50) or Sony MDR-7506 (~$100). Not open-back, not Bluetooth.
3. **Pop filter** (~$12) + **desk boom arm** (~$25).
4. **Two moving blankets** (~$25 each) and anything to hang them from.
5. **Wireless presentation clicker** (~$15) — see §5. Nobody mentions this and it's the difference between a fluid session and a broken one.

**Skip entirely:** audio interface, condenser mic, Cloudlifter, acoustic foam, plugin bundles. Episode-15 purchases at the earliest.

### The session
1. **Build the space.** Face into a corner. One blanket behind the mic (the surface you face), one behind your head. Towel on the desk between you and the mic. Mic on the boom arm, not resting on the desk — desk-clamped arms transmit every keyboard press.
2. **Clap test.** Clap once, hard, from your recording position. Ringing tail or fluttery "zing"? Add fabric, clap again. Stop when the clap sounds short and dead.
3. **Set position and mark it.** Mouth 4–6 inches from the capsule (fist with thumb extended = repeatable). Mic angled ~30° off your mouth line — **aim at your cheekbone, not your lips.** Pop filter 2 inches in front. **Tape the boom joints. Tape an X on the floor for your feet. Photograph the setup from two angles** — that photo is your rebuild instruction forever.
4. **Noise audit.** Record two minutes of silence, listen at high volume, write down everything you hear. Fix loudest first: AC off, computer moved, phone on airplane mode, window shut, fridge unplugged (set a timer to plug it back in).
5. **Set gain once.** Record your *loudest* planned line with real energy — not a mumbled test. Peaks at **−12 dBFS**. Write the gain number down.
6. **Calibration file.** Same 200-word passage six times, changing one variable each: 3in / 6in / 10in, straight-on / 30° off-axis, seated / standing. Listen on headphones. The winner is your permanent config.
7. **Plosive test.** *"Buy Bobby a big bottle of pop."* Look at the **waveform**, not just your ears. Low-frequency thumps → add 10° of angle, repeat.
8. **Reference slate.** One fixed sentence, the same one forever: *"This is the reference slate for [channel]."* Save as `slate_master.wav`. **Every future session starts by recording this and comparing to the master.** If they don't match, fix the setup *before* recording 3,000 words of mismatched audio.
9. **Record 60 seconds of room tone.** You'll need it to bridge gaps.

## 3. Writing so it can be said

The script is where narration is won. A sentence you can't say cleanly is a badly written sentence — rewrite it, don't perform around it.

- **One sentence per line.** Not paragraphs. This is what lets you see that your average sentence is 26 words.
- **15–19 words average.** All four reference channels land there, which at speaking rate is 5.4–6.0 seconds — and all four run **10–11 sentences per minute** regardless of subject. Any line over 25 words is a rewrite candidate. Any line needing two breaths gets split.
- **Contractions everywhere.** "It's", "didn't", "they'd". Without them you sound like a legal filing.
- **Front-load the subject.** Don't stack clauses before the main verb.
- **Open ~20% of sentences with a conjunction** — And / But / So / Yet / Now. This isn't sloppy grammar, it's a *vocal* device: a conjunction forces a continuation pitch contour instead of resetting to a fresh high pitch, which is what makes rapid graphic cuts feel like one unbroken thought instead of 200 separate announcements.
- **Round every number and never state one without a comparison.** "Avis lost half a billion" is a fact. *"Hertz lost $200 million, while Avis lost half a billion"* is a finding. Round to two significant digits — "$1.4 billion", never "$1,412,000,000", which is incomprehensible aloud and implies precision you can't defend.
- **Second person is a hard fork.** Kurzgesagt uses "you" 60× per 1,000 words; Wendover 1.3×. Both coherent. Drifting between them makes you sound like two people. **For us: Modern MBA's pattern** — use "you" in the cold open to make the problem personal, then withdraw to third person for the analysis. That's what makes it read as objective rather than sold.
- **Write the deictics in deliberately.** "This", "these", "here" are your hard sync points with the graphics — the visual must land *on that word*. Real Engineering uses them 27× per 1,000 words, roughly every 5 seconds. Target ~10–15 per 1,000 for business content. Then check the reverse: any graphic with no demonstrative pointing at it either gets one or gets cut.

**Final pass: read the whole thing aloud, unrecorded, at performance pace.** You're hunting four things — tongue-trippers, unknown pronunciations, places you ran out of air, and unspeakable sentences. Almost every retake in a first session is something this would have caught for free.

## 4. Marking up the script

Use one fixed legend, every time. Inventing symbols per script means decoding your own notation mid-read.

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| <u>underline</u> | operative word (the one carrying new information) |
| `/` | beat, ~0.5s |
| `//` | breath, ~1s |
| `~` | stretch this word |
| `○` | plosive risk — say it off-axis |
| `CAPS` | word stress on a multisyllabic term |
| `L / M / H` (margin) | pitch height for this paragraph |

**Mark in four separate passes, not all at once** — it's faster and far more accurate:
1. Operative words
2. Phrasing slashes and breaths
3. Pitch heights and inflection
4. Pronunciation and plosive risks

**Operative words: 2–3 per sentence maximum.** Never an article, preposition, or conjunction. Never a word whose meaning was already established in the previous sentence. Watch the modern habit of stressing pronouns ("WE believe", "THIS is why") when a real noun or verb is available. More than three underlines usually means the sentence should be two sentences.

**Breathe at thought boundaries, not at commas.** Punctuation is a writing convention, not a breathing plan.

## 5. Getting the words in front of your eyes

Unaddressed in most guides and the number one cause of avoidable retakes.

**Don't use paper** — it rustles into the mic and occupies a hand. **Don't put a laptop on the desk** — looking down compresses the larynx, which audibly darkens and constricts the voice, and it shortens your mic distance without you noticing.

- Script on a **tablet or second monitor at eye level**, behind and slightly above the mic, so your head stays on-axis.
- **18–24pt, 1.5 line spacing.**
- **One sentence per line. Never let a sentence break across a scroll boundary** — guaranteed retake.
- **Advance with the clicker**, one paragraph at a time. Manual beats auto-scroll, because auto-scroll sets your pace instead of you.

## 6. Delivery — the eight things that matter

1. **Pause BEFORE the payload, not after.** The single highest-leverage move for an incident-report format. A pause before creates anticipation; a pause after just creates dead air. *"The most important change wasn't the software. [beat] It was the process."*
2. **Emphasise with time and silence, not volume.** Slow slightly through the word, micro-pause before it, only a small volume bump. Volume alone raises pitch, tightens the throat, and reads as a sales pitch.
3. **Land every declarative sentence low.** Upspeak — rising at the end — turns statements into questions and reads as asking permission. Use the staircase: start high, step down in stages, small lift before the final word, brief pause, land low. It will feel over-flat to you and sound authoritative on playback. That gap *is* the calibration.
4. **Break your own cadence.** "Announcer voice" isn't a tone, it's *the same melodic contour repeated on consecutive sentences*. If three sentences in a row have the operative word in the same position, move one.
5. **Adopt the deadpan.** Analytical content has an inverse relationship between vocal energy and perceived trust. Keep your pitch range inside about a fifth. Enthusiasm implies you're selling; flatness implies the facts are doing the work. It's also the only register that survives 20 minutes. And it gives you headroom — when you finally do inflect on a reveal, two semitones reads as significant.
6. **But conversational ≠ low energy.** The commonest overcorrection is stripping investment out to avoid sounding announcery, landing on flat and disengaged. **Drill: record a paragraph normally, then again with visibly more investment at the same acoustic volume.** Energy up, volume flat. Take two usually sounds normal on playback.
7. **Deliver the reveal by slowing and dropping.** Cut the sentence under 10 words, 500ms pause before, drop ~2 semitones, slow ~15%, hard down-inflection, then 400–600ms of silence while the graphic holds. **This is the one place you're allowed dead air** — it works because the rest of the video has none.
8. **Stand. Gesture. Let your face move.** Standing opens breath support. A smile is audible — it raises high-frequency content. A frown genuinely makes the voice cold. Just keep your feet on the tape X and your head on-axis while your hands move.

**Two in-session resets, when a line won't work:**
- **Paraphrase reset.** Put the script down, say the idea in your own casual words as if to a friend, then immediately read the written line carrying that intention. Flips a stuck read instantly.
- **Change the listener.** Write at the top of every script who you're talking to. For us: *a smart friend who doesn't know this story yet and will be genuinely annoyed when they hear it.* Tone is downstream of relationship — chase a tone and you get a costume.

> **Rule: two failed takes triggers a reset, never a third attempt at the same wrong thing.**

## 7. The Lebanese English punch list

**Keep, no intelligibility cost — this is your character:** emphatic-consonant colouring, tapped or trilled /r/, clear /l/ where English uses dark /l/, t/d for "th".

⚠️ **Deliberately deprioritise "th".** Substituting /t/ and /d/ is intelligibility-safe internationally. (Avoid /s/ and /z/ substitutes — those are the confusing ones.) Don't spend attention here.

**Drill these — real cost, roughly a dozen items:**

| Target | Watch words |
|---|---|
| **/p/ vs /b/** — highest payoff | people, problem, police, point, power, pilot, report, pressure |
| **/dʒ/ vs /ʒ/** — the Levantine jim; needs the /d/ onset | just, job, judge, danger, change, bridge, engine, January |
| **/tʃ/ vs /ʃ/** — think /t/ released into /ʃ/ | chip/ship, chair/share, watch/wash |
| **/ŋ/** — no hard added /g/, no collapse to /n/ | thing, wrong, all -ing endings |
| **Aspirate /p t k/** at the start of stressed syllables | unaspirated "p" reads as "b" even if your lips did it right |
| **No epenthetic vowel in initial clusters** | "street" not "istreet" — build backwards: *reet → treet → street* |
| **Front-vowel squeeze /iː-ɪ-ɛ/** | drill *beat / bit / bet* as a triplet, not pairs |
| **/ɜr/** — no Arabic equivalent, constant in narration | bird, world, first, search, were, work |
| **Final consonants and grammatical endings** | plural -s, third-person -s, past-tense -ed |

**Aspiration test:** hold a sheet of paper in front of your mouth. "Pin" should move it, "bin" shouldn't.

**Vowel length is your free win.** English distinguishes leave/live, fool/full partly by quality but reliably by *duration* — and Arabic already has long/short vowel contrasts natively. You're leveraging an existing skill, not building one. The Lingua Franca Core keeps length and explicitly relaxes quality.

**Word stress matters more than phonemes.** Misplaced stress is more perceptually disruptive than a mispronounced sound — the listener simply can't find the word. Arabic stress follows syllable weight; English is lexical and unpredictable, so guessing misfires. Learn the suffix rules (`-tion/-ic/-ity/-ogy` → stress the syllable *before* the suffix: invesTIgation, teCHNOlogy, resPONsibility) and the noun/verb shifts (a REcord vs to reCORD, an INcrease vs to inCREASE). **Then reduce the unstressed syllables toward schwa** — giving every syllable full value is what hides the stress you placed correctly.

**Per episode: extract the 20–40 recurring multisyllabic terms and proper nouns, look up the stress, mark it in CAPS in the script.** For a business channel that's company names, executive names, and financial terms. Documentary work lives and dies on pronunciation research.

**Pause more, don't slow down.** Slowing every word destroys English rhythm, kills vowel reduction, and sounds condescending. Silence gives processing time without touching the rhythm — and reads as confidence rather than caution.

**Link inside thought groups, break between them.** "Took it out of" → *too-ki-tou-tof*. Linking is what sounds fluent, and it does clarity work: a final consonant attached to the next word's vowel is one you can't drop. Then stop linking at the group boundary — that silence is what defines the chunk.

## 8. Running the session

**Record section by section into separate files** — 150–250 words each, matching your script's section IDs. `E01_S03_take1.wav`. Say the section ID out loud at the top of each file.

### Mistake handling: flag-and-continue, not punch-and-roll

Most guides push punch-and-roll. **For a first-time narrator it's the wrong tool** — it makes you performer and engineer at once, and hunting for the key breaks your read worse than the flub did. Adopt it around episode 4.

**For now:**
1. Flub happens. **Don't stop recording.**
2. Pause two full seconds. **Clap once, hard.** Pause one second.
3. Re-read from the start of that sentence.
4. In the edit, delete backwards from each clap. The clap is a spike you can see from across the room.

**Three-attempt rule:** if a sentence fails three times, don't try a fourth. Paraphrase-reset it, or accept that the *sentence* is the problem and rewrite it on the spot. **Sentences that fail three times are almost always badly written, not badly read.**

**Fix:** wrong word, wrong number, wrong name, plosive thump, an obviously flat sentence, a stumble on a key term.
**Leave:** a slightly imperfect vowel, a breath, a mild pitch wobble, a phoneme you know you didn't nail. All invisible to the viewer, and each one you chase costs four minutes and adds tension to the next take.

### Stamina
- **40 minutes on, 15 off. Three to four blocks a day maximum** as a beginner. During breaks actually stop talking — vocal rest means silent.
- **Record in script order, then re-record the cold open at the end of the last block** without touching the mic.
- ⭐ **Energy decays and you won't notice.** At the top of every block, play back 30 seconds from the *first* block and match it. Without this, block three is 15% faster, flatter and quieter, and the video feels like it's getting worse as it goes.
- **Spot-check WPM at the end of each block.** Drifted more than 10% fast? Longer pauses, not slower words.
- **Stop for the day** at any of: repeated throat-clearing, involuntary pitch drop, pushing for volume, hoarseness. A strained voice takes 48 hours and costs you the week.

### Mouth noise
Hydrate **hours** before, not minutes — chugging water at the mic does almost nothing. **A slice of Granny Smith apple** ten minutes before; the malic acid stimulates saliva. **Breathe through your nose between takes** — mouth-breathing dries you out faster than anything and guarantees clicks by hour two.

### Warm-up (10 minutes, load-bearing)
4-7-8 breathing ×3 → diaphragmatic breathing ×5 → **straw phonation** 20–30s (hum through a narrow straw, sliding up and down — the safest single warm-up in voice science) → lip trills 15–30s → hums on m/n opening into "ah" → **sirens** ×5 slow (maps your range, the standard antidote to monotone) → tongue twisters ×3–5, clarity over speed.

Skipping it costs you the first ten minutes of the take, which you re-record anyway.

## 9. Post-processing

**Order matters. Do the silence pass LAST**, after all editing, never on the raw file.

| Gap type | Target |
|---|---|
| Inter-sentence | **350–500ms** — trim to this, don't go below |
| Section boundaries | **800–1000ms** — protect manually |
| Auto-trim threshold | only silence **>1.5s**, truncate to 500ms |

⚠️ **Do not run a blanket "trim everything over 500ms" pass.** It chops breath tails leaving clicky joins, flattens your deliberate phrasing, and produces a machine-gun rhythm that reads as AI-generated — the exact thing this audience hunts for. **Leave breath sounds in at low level;** removing them entirely is what makes narration sound synthetic.

⚠️ **Don't use Adobe Podcast Enhance as a routine step.** It works by re-synthesising speech, which flattens exactly the tonal character you're deliberately keeping, and the v2 model has documented complaints about robotic output. Emergency rescue only. **Fix the room instead** — cheaper and permanent.

**On the noise floor:** the −60 dBFS bar is an *audiobook submission* standard, not a YouTube one. Aim at it, but with a music bed under the narration, −50 dBFS is inaudible to a viewer. **Don't spend three weekends chasing a fridge hum instead of shipping episode one.**

## 10. What this actually costs ⚠️

The number that makes people quit after episode two.

| | Ep 1–3 | Ep 4–10 | Ep 10+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setup + warm-up | 30 min | 20 min | 15 min |
| Recording (per 15 min finished) | **3–5 hrs** | 1.5–2.5 hrs | 45–60 min |
| Cleanup edit (per 15 min finished) | **2–4 hrs** | 1.5–2 hrs | 45–90 min |
| **Narration total** | **6–9 hrs** | 3.5–5 hrs | 2–3 hrs |

Narration only — no research, writing, graphics, or picture edit.

> ### ⭐ Episode one should be 10–12 minutes, not 20–25. About 1,600 words.
> A 3,000-word first attempt takes two days and produces something you hate. Scale up once the workflow is automatic. **Budget a full day for episode one's narration** — if you expect two hours you'll quit.

Most of the improvement lands between episodes 3 and 8.

**And: you will hate your own voice on playback.** This is universal and peaks in the first three sessions. It is not information about your voice.

## 11. Changes this forces elsewhere

- **[channel-bible.md](channel-bible.md) §6** says 15–25 min. That stays the format target, but **episode 001 is 10–12 min**.
- **Script length is now a hard number**, not a vibe: word count = target minutes × 150.
- **One sentence = one shot is not survivable solo.** 200 graphic beats per episode is a studio workload. Realistic: **40–60 distinct visual states per 20 minutes**, each held 15–30s with internal movement (slow push, sequential reveal, highlight-on-cue). So write in **paragraph-sized visual blocks** and mark the 3–5 sync words inside each block where something must land. This directly shapes the Remotion component list — we need components that *hold and evolve*, not one-shot cards.
- **The deictic count is a script QA check.** Every "this/these/here" is a hard sync frame.
