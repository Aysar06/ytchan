# Transcript 07 — vidIQ
**Topic:** Six reasons good videos get ignored — the biases operating *before* the click
**Saved:** 2026-07-25

---

This quarter represents the choice everybody makes when they see a video on YouTube. A simple coin flip: **should I watch this video or not?**

Have you ever uploaded something you put your blood, sweat and tears into, only to watch it go nowhere? Then you've probably felt like the game is rigged. A lot of people instantly blame the algorithm. But **the algorithm is actually the last thing standing between you and people landing on heads and clicking your video.** The first thing standing in the way is **the person watching.** They're making a judgment call about your video **before they've even clicked on it.** They have no information about the quality of your content, and yet they've made a choice.

There are six reasons good videos get ignored. We'll start with the one that kills most channels before they've had a chance.

---

## 1. Familiarity bias — strangers get swiped past

Think about Charlie — MoistCr1TiKaL, penguinz0. A new viewer sees a **plain thumbnail: a person sitting in a room in a white t-shirt.** If they click anyway, they find exactly what they expected — a guy in his room in a white t-shirt talking about whatever's going on that day on the internet. And he gets **3 million views in 24 hours.**

Now flip it. You're a new creator publishing your second, third, fourth video. You've done animation, editing, built your transitions, color graded it. **It gets 52 views.** Compared to Charlie, the contrast feels stark.

But that's YouTube. **Nobody watches Charlie's videos going "oh my gosh, the production value here is incredible."** They watch because **he's familiar to them. It's comfortable. It's automatic.**

> That new creator is losing **not because their video is worse than Charlie's.** They're losing because **they're a stranger.** And on YouTube, strangers don't get the benefit of the doubt. They get swiped past.

**The fix:** you can't fake familiarity, but you can build it — **consistently.** Every time you post, that's another deposit into the trust account. The more they see your face, hear your voice, and recognize your style, the more familiar you become. Charlie started at zero like everybody else, but he showed up again and again until watching him became a **habit.** Unfortunately, that's the only way through this one.

## 2. Trend bias — the wave doesn't care who you are

January 2024. Target releases a limited edition pink Valentine's Day Stanley cup. What happens next is one of the most unintentionally unhinged things the internet has ever produced: **fully grown adults trampling each other in the aisles of Target for a pink water bottle.**

That one moment created a wave so big **it didn't matter who you were or how many subscribers you had.** Different creators, different angles, different subscriber counts — hauls, rants, essays, drama, reviews, shorts — all riding the exact same wave, landing millions of views and creating outliers for themselves. Whether your channel was about sustainability, drama, or lifestyle vlogs, **this trend was carrying you.**

**You don't have to choose between trends and quality. The smartest creators use trends as a vehicle.** Two ways:

**A. Expertise first.**
- **LegalEagle** is a real practicing lawyer. When a massive trial breaks the news cycle, he doesn't scramble to become relevant overnight — **he's already a lawyer, he's already relevant.** He attaches existing expertise to the moment and **lets the trend carry the distribution while his credibility does the rest.**
- **Dr. Todd Grande**, a mental health counselor with over a million subscribers, **doesn't even chase trends.** He shows up so consistently on psychology, personality and human behavior that when the Amber Heard trial takes over the internet or Kanye's behavior becomes a cultural conversation, **the algorithm finds him.**

**B. Pure timing.**
- **Peak Platinums** — platinum trophy hunting, a crowded niche. **6,000 subscribers, 58 videos.** When the Spider-Man *Brand New Day* trailer dropped and the internet lost its mind, **they already had a Spider-Man platinum video ready.** 39,000 views.
- *"I survived 100 days in Avatar in Minecraft"* — posted right as Avatar 3 dominated pop culture. **The game didn't change. The format didn't change. It was just timing.** 790,000 views on a 97,000-subscriber channel.

> Find a trending moment, attach your genuine expertise to it, and **let the trend handle the distribution while your content handles the retention.**

## 3. Recency bias — time is working against your video

YouTube published data on how daily views change as a video ages:

| Age of a how-to video | Avg. daily views |
|---|---|
| Brand new | **88** |
| 1 year old | **7.7** |
| 3 years old | **4** |

**A 95% drop in daily reach just because the video got older.** That's not theory — YouTube confirmed it in their own documentation. **The algorithm favors recency**, especially with shorts.

Think about "how to do the perfect push-up." The human body hasn't had a software update in a while. The biomechanics haven't changed since before YouTube was invented. But if you searched and saw a masterclass from 2016, you'd ignore it in favor of a more recent video — because you saw the little grey **"10 years ago"** timestamp. If the perfectly average competing video says **3 weeks ago**, you're more likely to click that one. **We are psychologically conditioned to view anything old as obsolete.** It doesn't matter if the lesson is about literal biology.

Big creators know this, and you've seen the trick: *"How to start a YouTube channel in 2025."* You click, something smells off, and the video was actually uploaded in 2022. The creator just logs in every year and updates the title — 2023, 2024, 2025. (**We're guilty of this too.**) Sometimes it makes sense: if the information hasn't expired, saying "in 2025" is accurate, and *not* changing it means people scroll right past.

**Three fixes:**
1. **Be careful with your language.** Saying *"right now in 2026"* or *"this year"* puts an automatic expiration date on the content. **If your language is timeless, the content stays relevant longer.**
2. **Update the old video** — new title, fresh thumbnail. Show viewers and YouTube that it's still relevant.
3. **(Recommended) Make a new video on the topic** — if there's something new to say. Figure out why that video did so well, **read the comments on the old video**, and make the next version: update the title, tighten the pacing, add new information that fills the gaps you found in those comments. **This keeps recency bias from being your opponent — you're working with it.**

## 4. Validation bias — people want *their* answer, not the best answer

Think about the last time you were about to make an objectively terrible decision — buying something you couldn't afford, contacting an ex. Before you did, you asked someone for advice. **Did you choose the person who'd give you good advice, or the one you were pretty confident would tell you what you wanted to hear?**

We love it when our feelings are validated. **We're not searching for the best answer. We're searching for our answer.** When a video tells people the opposite of what they want to hear, they're likely not to click. And **YouTube and social algorithms are actively helping them do this.**

We have data. Using the **vidIQ MCP attached to Claude**, we looked at a sample of **137 million YouTube videos from 2025–2026** and how title framing affected views. Median views:

| Title framing | Median views | vs. baseline |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral how-to (pure info, no opinion) | **650** | baseline |
| *"Thing is back" / "we were right"* | **1,215** | ~1.9× |
| *"Thing is dead / dying / ruining something"* | **1,371** | ~2.1× |
| ⭐ ***"I was wrong about thing"*** | **3,181** | **~4.9×** |

Why does *"thing is dead"* double the baseline? Because plenty of viewers already felt that way, and your video **confirmed what was in their gut.** *"Thing is back / we were right"* is the same idea in reverse — you're telling somebody the thing they already believe is actually true, **they're not crazy**, and now they feel vindicated.

But *"I was wrong about thing"* obliterates everything else. It works for **anyone who thought the mainstream opinion was wrong and has been waiting for somebody, anybody, to finally say it out loud.**

> It kind of sucks, but **the most clicked videos on YouTube aren't the most informative ones. They're the most validating ones.**

**What to do:** **Don't be dishonest, but pick a lane. Lead with your conclusion, not your question.** Package the video around a belief someone already holds, or one they've been waiting for you to challenge. **All the nuance in the world can live inside the video** — but the title and thumbnail, the front door, need to be clear. **It needs to be a door for somebody specifically.**

## 5. View count bias — the restaurant with the line

Two similar restaurants side by side, similar prices. One has a line out the door and every table full. The other is a ghost town. Which are you choosing? Most people are curious about the one with the line.

That's happening with every viewer scanning a page of videos. **High view count → must be worth watching. Low view count → I don't know if I want to take the risk.** Not because anyone assessed the quality, but because **a number told them nobody's watching this.**

It affects which **channels** people trust, too — and some creators exploit it. **These are real YouTube channels. Look at their names. Those aren't view counts — those are channel names**, designed to appear in search results looking exactly like view counts do in the YouTube interface. If you're not paying attention, your brain processes the number and the timestamp as **social proof** before you've registered what you're looking at. A calculated exploit. *(I do not agree with or condone this tactic — in my opinion this behavior should be bannable.)*

**The nuance: view count bias is content-dependent.** A breaking news story from a channel I don't know with 800 views, released hours ago? I'll wait for a larger channel I already trust — **the stakes are high.** But a fun challenge, a cool recipe, a funny reaction, with a thumbnail that works and a title that makes sense? **I may click regardless of view count** — the stakes are low, it's not going to influence my opinion on a world event.

## 6. Length bias — mismatched length is the enemy

Stick a 20-minute deep dive in front of somebody who wants a YouTube short: they're in for three seconds and gone. Give a 60-second tutorial to somebody who needs a full 15-minute breakdown: **they feel cheated. You've addressed the wrong room.**

On our own channel:
- A **60-second short**, one YouTube setting tip → **3.1 million views**
- A **15-minute** full breakdown of 16 settings, genuinely useful → **354,000 views**

**Nine times fewer views on the video that took much, much longer to make.**

Yes, shorts get distributed differently — the algorithm pushes them to a completely different audience. So let's flip it. **Kurzgesagt** is an animated science channel with **24 million subscribers.** Their videos run 10 to 20 minutes. Dense, deep, no shortcuts. They consistently pull 2, 5, 20, sometimes **50 million views** per video. If Kurzgesagt pushed a 60-second short on the Fermi paradox and left it there, **their audience would feel cheated.** People watch that channel for the full deep dive — the music, the animation, **the feeling of actually understanding something complex** by the end.

> **Length isn't the enemy. Mismatched length is.**

**And a point about watch time specifically:** if a viewer sits through an entire 15-minute video, **that viewer is exponentially more valuable** than someone who watched a 60-second short. This **27-minute video has 43,000 views** — modest on the surface — but it generated **5,200 hours of watch time.** That was huge for the whole channel, because those people **clicked and stayed**, which is a really strong signal to YouTube that this is content worth pushing. It also nearly guarantees those viewers saw our next upload — they're in the watch history, and **YouTube knows full well that when they watch our content, they sit down and watch for quite a long time.**

**Before you decide on length, look at what's working in your niche.** How long do those videos run? Shorts or long form? **That's what viewers have come to expect from content in this space.**
